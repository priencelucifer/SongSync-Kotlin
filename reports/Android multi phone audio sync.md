# Measure the speaker, not the player

SongSync's sync engine already follows the same pattern as Snapcast and AirPlay: one shared clock, a timeline published by the host, starts scheduled a few hundred milliseconds ahead, and closed-loop correction. Its remaining error comes from three places the engine cannot see. The first is how ExoPlayer reports position: in whole milliseconds, smoothed, and with the AudioTrack hidden. The second is constant content offsets when phones fetch different files of "the same" song. The third is how acoustic calibration handles the distance between each phone and the host's microphone. The systems that reach sub-millisecond accuracy all do one thing SongSync does not: they decide which sample plays at which instant inside the audio output path, using hardware presentation timestamps, and they correct drift by adding or dropping single samples at no more than 0.05%. Snapcast reports deviation "typically below 0.2 ms" this way ([Snapcast](https://github.com/badaix/snapcast)). Clock sync is not the bottleneck: NTP-style exchanges between Android phones over Wi-Fi land within ±1 ms in more than 85% of attempts ([NESL](https://github.com/nesl/Time-Sync-Across-Smartphones)). Perception sets the target at ≤5 ms as good and ≤1–2 ms as excellent. Echo starts around 7–8 ms for equal-loudness music ([Brown et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4310855)), and geometry adds 2.9 ms per metre of path difference, which no software can remove for every listener. The plan, in order: measure where real-phone error comes from; pin byte-identical streams and fix the calibration's distance bias (quick wins); get sub-millisecond position from the audio sink and add clock-skew tracking (medium); and finally build a sample-accurate AudioTrack/Oboe output path, with host streaming as an optional mode (big rewrite).

## Ears set the target at 1 to 5 ms, and geometry sets the floor

Several phones playing the same material at similar loudness are the textbook lead/lag loudspeaker case, and that case has the lowest echo thresholds. Below about 1 ms, listeners hear **summing localization**: one fused image placed between the sources. From about 1 to 10 ms the sound stays fused but pulls toward the earliest speaker (the precedence effect). For speech and music at equal amplitude, **the echo threshold is 7–8 ms**, rising to 15–26 ms only when the late source is realistically quieter ([Brown, Stecker & Tollin 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4310855)). For clicks, the threshold is 2–10 ms ([PMC2677267](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2677267)). Practitioner summaries put delays under about 5 ms in the "coloration" (comb-filter) zone, 5–30 ms in a reverb-like zone, and more than 30 ms as a distinct echo ([QSC](https://blogs.qsc.com/live-sound/?p=2823)). Vendor patents state the same ladder as engineering targets: **10 ms across rooms, 1 ms in the same room, one sample (~20 µs) for a stereo pair** ([US 11914921](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11914921)). AmpMe's founders say "if it's 10 milliseconds off, you can hear it" ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/ampme-plans-to-kill-bluetooth-speakers-by-syncing-music-between-smartphones)). Lip-sync tolerance (+45/−125 ms) is far looser and never the binding constraint ([ITU-R BT.1359](https://itu.int/dms_pubrec/itu-r/rec/bt/R-REC-BT.1359-0-199802-S!!PDF-E.pdf)).

That gives SongSync four working tiers: **≤1–2 ms excellent, ≤5 ms good, ≤10 ms acceptable, >20 ms audible doubling on transients**. Physics adds a floor that software cannot remove. At about 343 m/s, each metre of path difference adds about 2.9 ms ([arXiv 2512.10778](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10778) gives the 21 µs per-sample time-of-flight scale this follows from). Phones spread 3 m apart therefore differ by up to about 8.7 ms for a listener standing next to one of them. This changes priorities. Once device-latency error reaches about 1 ms, further gains are audible only near the point where the phones are equidistant. Getting from today's few milliseconds down to about 1 ms is worthwhile. Getting from 1 ms down to 20 µs is not, for phones spread around a room.

## Every accurate system timestamps audio against one clock

The working systems converge on one design. **Snapcast** has its server tag each PCM chunk with server time. Each client keeps a server-clock offset as the median of the last 200 measurements ([time_provider.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/badaix/snapcast/develop/client/time_provider.cpp)) and plays each chunk a fixed buffer later. Inside every Oboe callback, the client asks the hardware when the next written frame will actually be heard (`getTimestamp(CLOCK_MONOTONIC)` plus the frames still queued) and fills the buffer with exactly the samples, or silence, that belong at that instant ([oboe_player.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/badaix/snapcast/develop/client/player/oboe_player.cpp)). Drift correction starts at **100 µs** of error and inserts or drops one frame every N frames, capped at a **0.05% rate change**. Hard resync happens only when the long median exceeds 2 ms, the short median exceeds 5 ms, or the mini median exceeds 50 ms ([stream.cpp](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/badaix/snapcast/develop/client/stream.cpp)). The result is deviation "typically below 0.2 ms" ([Snapcast README](https://github.com/badaix/snapcast)).

**AirPlay 2** (as implemented by shairport-sync) uses a PTP-derived shared clock, aligned "usually to within a fraction of a millisecond", and about 0.5 s of playout latency. It ignores errors below **2 ms** (`drift_tolerance_in_seconds`), corrects with interpolation or soxr resampling, and hard-resyncs at 50 ms. It also exposes a fixed per-device backend latency offset ([README](https://github.com/mikebrady/shairport-sync/blob/master/README.md), [conf](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mikebrady/shairport-sync/master/scripts/shairport-sync.conf)). **LE Audio** pushes the same idea down to the radio: every sink renders each SDU at a shared presentation point, synchronized to within 20 µs ([US10652659](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10652659), [Nordic](https://nrfconnectdocs.nordicsemi.com/ncs/latest/nrfxlib/softdevice_controller/doc/isochronous_channels.html)). Apps cannot reach that link-layer clock. **Sonos, Cast and Echo** publish no accuracy figures. Google Home still ships a manual "group delay correction" slider ([Chromestory](https://chromestory.com/2016/12/chromecast-audio-sync-fix/)), which shows that even large vendors cannot measure every output's latency from software. **AmpMe**, the closest analogue to SongSync, measures each phone's real output offset acoustically with ultrasonic tones and trains a per-model offset predictor ([VentureBeat](https://venturebeat.com/ai/ampme-plans-to-kill-bluetooth-speakers-by-syncing-music-between-smartphones), [NVIDIA](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/?p=7153)). Spotify Jam does not do multi-phone playback at all ([Uproxx](https://uproxx.com/music/how-to-use-spotify-jam/)).

Compared with these systems, SongSync already has the shared timeline, a scheduled lead of 400–1500 ms (similar to AirPlay 2's 0.5 s), median filtering, a dead-band with hysteresis, and acoustic calibration. It differs in three ways. **Its correction is about 40× coarser**: speed steps of 0.25% up to ±2% (20 ms/s), against Snapcast's 0.05% cap (0.5 ms/s). **Its dead-band is 4 ms**, against 0.1 ms (Snapcast) and 2 ms (AirPlay). And **its measurement is ExoPlayer's `currentPosition`**, not the hardware timestamp. Because Media3's Sonic path stretches time while keeping pitch, the ±2% nudges do not cause the roughly 34-cent pitch shift a raw rate change would. The real costs are time-stretch processing on every nudge and a correction granularity that cannot settle below a few milliseconds.

## Phone clocks sync to about 1 ms over Wi-Fi

The best phone-to-phone study found NTP-style sync on Android stayed **within ±1 ms in more than 85% of Wi-Fi attempts**, and within (−1, +1) ms 95% of the time on campus Wi-Fi. Over BLE the figures were **±3 ms in more than 85% of attempts** and ±5 ms in 90%. Using the audio peripheral as a side channel reached **±200 µs in 86%** of experiments ([NESL](https://github.com/nesl/Time-Sync-Across-Smartphones), [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/336954458_Exploiting_Smartphone_Peripherals_for_Precise_Time_Synchronization)). SongSync's estimator, the median offset of the fastest quarter of a 20–60 sample window, belongs to the right family. Filtering for minimum RTT is the defense that Huygens formalizes as "coded probes" ([NSDI '18](https://www.usenix.org/conference/nsdi18/presentation/geng)). It matters especially because **802.11 power save can defer downlink frames by one or more 100 ms beacon intervals**, and that delay is asymmetric, which breaks NTP's symmetric-delay assumption ([dot11zen](https://dot11zen.blogspot.com/2018/02/80211-power-management-with-packet.html)).

Two gaps remain in SongSync's clock layer. The first is **skew**. Phone clocks drift at roughly 1–80 ppm, a different rate on each device ([NTUST](https://faculty.csie.ntust.edu.tw/~weichung/documents/DIPDMWC2016.pdf)), and a 10 ppm crystal gains more than 1 ms per minute ([Fraunhofer](https://websites.fraunhofer.de/smart-sensing-insights/clock-drifts-oscillator-tolerances-in-embedded-systems/)). A 20-sample window at 1 Hz estimates the offset as it stood about 10 s ago. At 80 ppm relative drift, that lag is up to about 0.8 ms of systematic error. Fitting offset against time over the low-RTT samples (a linear fit or a two-state Kalman filter) removes the lag and predicts the offset between probes. The second gap is **radio state**. Android 10+ `WIFI_MODE_FULL_LOW_LATENCY` explicitly disables power save. It applies only while the app is in the foreground, the screen is on, and Wi-Fi has internet access ([AOSP](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/wifi-low-latency)). SongSync's phones do have internet, because each one streams, and `NON_DISRUPTIVE` keeps them on their normal Wi-Fi. The lock will therefore help during foreground use, although the app does not acquire any Wi-Fi lock today. Nearby Connections publishes no latency or throughput figures. It upgrades from Bluetooth to Wi-Fi opportunistically, and developers report links stuck at "Bluetooth speed" ([google/nearby](https://github.com/google/nearby/blob/main/connections/README.md), [connectivity-samples #140](https://github.com/android/connectivity-samples/issues/140)). The diagnostics should record which medium each phone's clock estimate came from, and the estimator should discard pre-upgrade samples when `onBandwidthChanged` fires. Today SongSync only fires a new burst at that point and keeps the old samples.

Clock error is not where most of the error sits. On Wi-Fi it is about ±1 ms, inside the "good" tier. The larger and less visible errors sit between the timeline and the air.

## ExoPlayer hides the timestamps that sample-accurate sync needs

Android does expose acoustic presentation time. `AudioTrack.getTimestamp()` and `AAudioStream_getTimestamp` return a (frame, nanoTime) pair on `CLOCK_MONOTONIC`, documented as "the time at which a particular frame was played on a speaker or headset" ([NDK AAudio](https://developer.android.com/ndk/reference/group/audio)). The CDD **requires ±2 ms output timestamp accuracy and strongly recommends ±1 ms** ([CDD 5.6 mirror](https://os.unplugged.com/werunplugged/unplugged-vendor/raw/branch/main/compatibility/cdd/5_multimedia/5_6_audio-latency.md), [CDD commit](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/compatibility/cdd/+/f845c6c%5E%21/)). Vendor HALs have shipped timestamp bugs ([Qualcomm HAL fix](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/hardware/qcom/audio/+/680f560%5E%21/)). Bluetooth paths carry the headset's AVDTP delay report only when the sink reports it and the Bluetooth audio HAL v2+ forwards it ([a2dp_transport.h](https://git.aicp-rom.com/packages_modules_Bluetooth.git/plain/system/audio_hal_interface/aidl/a2dp_transport.h), [HAL v2 commit](https://gerrit.omnirom.org/plugins/gitiles/android_hardware_interfaces/+/1ea96a3802618c707933665e84576ae11d40bc2a)). VLC found getTimestamp returning false on Bluetooth on older devices ([VLC](https://mailman.videolan.org/pipermail/vlc-commits/2015-April/030027.html)). The javadoc advises polling only every 10–60 s once the stream is stable, and building a high-resolution frame↔time mapping at app level, because "short-term differences between successive timestamp reports are not meaningful" ([archived docs](https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20160706144530mp_/https://developer.android.com/reference/android/media/AudioTrack.html)).

Media3 consumes these timestamps internally. It fast-polls every 10 ms and slow-polls every 10 s. It treats timestamps as advancing only when consecutive ones agree within 1 ms, and rejects them as spurious when they are more than 5 s off the system clock ([AudioTimestampPoller](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/release/libraries/exoplayer/src/main/java/androidx/media3/exoplayer/audio/AudioTimestampPoller.java)). Until timestamps advance, it falls back to a smoothed playhead estimate ([AudioTrackPositionTracker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/release/libraries/exoplayer/src/main/java/androidx/media3/exoplayer/audio/AudioTrackPositionTracker.java)). This explains SongSync's earlier real-device bug, "some phones report wrong positions right after start", and why it needs a 1 s settle period. What the app sees is `Player.currentPosition`: **millisecond-quantised, smoothed, and sampled on the main thread**. The follower then medians 10 of those readings and corrects only beyond 4 ms. Below about 2–4 ms, SongSync cannot tell whether a phone is really off or whether the measurement is noisy. Its own simulation shows the problem: the sim's reported position is the true position plus unbiased ±1.5 ms noise, so the "steady state ≤ 4.4 ms" result assumes that ExoPlayer's position equals the acoustic position. On real phones, any output delay after the timestamp point, such as speaker DSP or protection, sits outside that measurement entirely ([AOSP SRC](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/src)).

Two hooks lead to better accuracy. The incremental one keeps ExoPlayer. `DefaultAudioSink.Builder.setAudioProcessors()` inserts custom processors ahead of Sonic, and the sink path can be wrapped ([DefaultAudioSink](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/release/libraries/exoplayer/src/main/java/androidx/media3/exoplayer/audio/DefaultAudioSink.java)). A forwarding sink that records each `getCurrentPositionUs()` result with the `System.nanoTime()` at which the playback thread read it gives the follower microsecond-resolution, timestamp-derived positions without main-thread jitter. This is an engineering inference: no source in the notes measures it. A frame-dropping or frame-duplicating processor would change frame counts that Media3 maps through its chain, and that risks position glitches. Media3 also calls the platform `PlaybackParams` speed path "device-dependent, less reliable" ([DefaultAudioSink](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/release/libraries/exoplayer/src/main/java/androidx/media3/exoplayer/audio/DefaultAudioSink.java)), which matches SongSync's 2.0.3 finding that AudioTrack-param speed changes glitch. The thorough option takes ownership of the output. ExoPlayer or MediaCodec decodes to PCM, and the app writes it to its own AudioTrack (Kotlin) or an Oboe stream (NDK). The app writes silence until the frame index that `getTimestamp` says will be heard at the scheduled host time, then corrects by inserting or dropping single frames, where one frame at 48 kHz is about 20.8 µs. Oboe gives MMAP/exclusive low-latency streams. Low latency is not what sync needs, though. Sync needs a stable latency that is reported correctly. Oboe's `getTimestamp` is unimplemented on the OpenSL ES backend below API 27 ([Oboe FullGuide](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/google/oboe/main/docs/FullGuide.md)), which matters because SongSync's minSdk is 26. This is exactly Snapcast's Android player, and it removes the "learned start latency" heuristic altogether, because the start becomes a frame index rather than a guessed `play()` lead.

## Different files can hide 6 to 118 ms of constant offset

Two phones whose `currentPosition` readings match can still emit different audio, because lossy codecs prepend priming samples. **AAC-LC typically adds 2112 samples (47.9 ms at 44.1 kHz)**. Depending on the encoder this can be 1024, 2624, or **5186 for HE-AAC (≈118 ms)** ([AAC.js wiki](https://github.com/uupaa/AAC.js/wiki/EncoderDelay), [hydrogenaudio](https://hydrogenaudio.org/index.php?msg=787664)). **Opus pre-skip is typically 312 samples (6.5 ms)** ([RFC 7845](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc7845), [FFmpeg #6841](https://lists.ffmpeg.org/pipermail/ffmpeg-trac/2017-December/042220.html)). Media3 trims priming from MP4 edit lists and iTunSMPB. It does so only within `MAX_GAPLESS_TRIM_SIZE_SAMPLES = 4`, and for fragmented MP4 it supports only a single edit, applied as a timestamp shift ([BoxParser](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/main/libraries/extractor/src/main/java/androidx/media3/extractor/mp4/BoxParser.java), [FragmentedMp4Extractor](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/main/libraries/extractor/src/main/java/androidx/media3/extractor/mp4/FragmentedMp4Extractor.java)). The same bytes decoded by the same Media3 version trim identically. **Different files are where constant offsets come from.**

The one independent YouTube measurement found that itag 140 (AAC, 44.1 kHz) and itag 251 (Opus, 48 kHz) of the same upload are "not time-aligned with each other, or with the source". They are 20–60 ms longer than the source, and the Opus stream was exactly 312 samples late ([audiomisc.co.uk](https://audiomisc.co.uk/YouTube/SpotTheDifference.html)). That matters for SongSync's code. `YouTubeSource.audioUrl()` independently picks the highest-`averageBitrate` progressive stream **on each phone**, and the `Track` carries only the video id. NewPipe can hand different phones different stream lists, so two phones can play 140 and 251 with a constant offset that the closed loop never sees and that recurs on every song. JioSaavn is already pinned: the host verifies the bitrate and ships `bitrateKbps` in the `Track`. JioSaavn's encoder and container metadata per bitrate are undocumented, so the 96 kbps tier could be HE-AAC while 160/320 are AAC-LC. Pinning removes that risk only if all phones honor the same tier, which they do today. The fix is to remove the variable. The host pins the exact YouTube itag (and ideally the content length) the same way it pins JioSaavn bitrate, and each phone logs the selected `Format.encoderDelay`, `encoderPadding`, `sampleRate`, `codecs` and decoder name ([GaplessInfoHolder](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/androidx/media/main/libraries/extractor/src/main/java/androidx/media3/extractor/GaplessInfoHolder.java)), so any mismatch shows up immediately. Cross-correlating decoded PCM recovers such offsets to the sample: the YouTube study found its 312-sample offset with a 3.75 s window. That is the safety net if pinning is ever impossible.

## Acoustic calibration fixes the last mile but measures geometry too

SongSync 2.1.0's echo calibration is a one-way version of **BeepBeep**. BeepBeep reached 1–2 cm ranging accuracy (about 30–60 µs) on 2007 phones through "two-way sensing, self-recording, and sample counting", which cancels unknown OS latency ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=155459), [paper](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/chunyi/pubs/sensys106-beepbeep.pdf)). SongSync's design choices match the literature. It uses the host's own chirps to cancel the microphone path. It uses an analytic-envelope matched filter with first-arrival detection rather than the global maximum, because reverberation defeats plain correlation peaks ([ExpresSense](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2301.06762)). It takes a median of four chirps per phone. It uses UNPROCESSED when `PROPERTY_SUPPORT_AUDIO_SOURCE_UNPROCESSED` is non-null, which the CDD guarantees free of AGC, high-pass filtering and echo cancellation, with ±10 dB response from 100 Hz to 7 kHz ([CDD 5.11](https://os.unplugged.com/werunplugged/unplugged-vendor/raw/branch/main/compatibility/cdd/5_multimedia/5_11_unprocessed-audio.md)). Two points need attention. First, the VOICE_RECOGNITION fallback is only recommended to be flat from 100 Hz to 4 kHz ([CDD 5.4](https://os.unplugged.com/werunplugged/unplugged-vendor/raw/branch/main/compatibility/cdd/5_multimedia/5_4_audio-recording.md)), so half of the 2–8 kHz chirp may be attenuated on phones without UNPROCESSED. Second, input timestamps are only good to ±2 ms (MUST) ([CDD 5.6](https://os.unplugged.com/werunplugged/unplugged-vendor/raw/branch/main/compatibility/cdd/5_multimedia/5_6_audio-latency.md)). The analyzer correctly uses them only for the coarse ±600 ms window and relies on sample counting for fine timing.

The calibration measures arrival time **at the host's microphone**, which includes acoustic flight time. A phone 2 m from the host is corrected about 5.8 ms earlier than a phone 0.3 m away, and that bakes geometry into a per-route constant that is persisted and reused after the phones move. Mic-based calibration patents choose deliberately between equalizing arrival at a listener position and equalizing emission ([US 11477596](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11477596), [US 9900692](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9900692)). SongSync should make the same choice explicitly. The cheap version is UX: put all phones next to the host during calibration, or hold the host at the listening spot and accept a placement-specific result that is not persisted per route. The thorough version is BeepBeep two-way ranging: every phone also records, distances are recovered, and flight time is subtracted so that phones emit simultaneously. A second, smaller caveat: the calibration track is 48 kHz PCM WAV, while music is typically 44.1 kHz AAC. Android resampling has "minimal and consistent" but unquantified delay ([AOSP SRC](https://source.android.com/docs/core/audio/src)), so a correction measured on the WAV path may not transfer exactly to the music path. Measuring that difference is one of the open questions. Phone DAC clocks also differ by tens of ppm, so a one-off acoustic correction stays valid only as long as continuous drift correction holds the phones together, which SongSync's closed loop already does.

## Host streaming buys identical samples at the cost of the link

Snapcast's sub-millisecond result relies partly on centralization: one decode at the host, so every client plays identical samples. Streaming Opus from the SongSync host would guarantee identical content, let guests without internet join, and make codec delay (26.5 ms by default ([Opus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opus_(audio_format)))) a constant that cancels across receivers. The costs are concrete. Nearby BYTES payloads cap at 32 KB ([overview](https://developers.google.com/nearby/connections/overview)). The transport is reliable and ordered, so a retransmission stalls everything behind it. Pre-upgrade BLE links move only "tens of KB/s" ([quickshare PR](https://github.com/mtch3n/quickshare/pull/3)). The host sends N unicast copies: 128 kbps × 7 guests ≈ 0.9 Mbps, which fits Wi-Fi but not a struggling Bluetooth link. SongSync also deliberately uses `NON_DISRUPTIVE` connections, which limit Nearby's ability to upgrade to a hotspot or Wi-Fi Direct. A 0.5–2 s jitter buffer, in line with AirPlay's 0.5 s and Snapcast's `bufferMs`, is required ([Snapcast protocol](https://github.com/badaix/snapcast/blob/develop/doc/binary_protocol.md)). On the encoder side, Android exposes an Opus encoder through `MediaRecorder` and Codec2 only from API 29 ([MediaRecorder.AudioEncoder](https://developer.android.com/reference/kotlin/android/media/MediaRecorder.AudioEncoder), [C2SoftOpusEnc](https://gerrit.omnirom.org/plugins/gitiles/android_frameworks_av/+/c237cbc63980210d4b1c083cd0450cc7bf0b2606%5E%21)). With minSdk 26, NDK libopus is the portable choice ([libopusencoder-android](https://github.com/vector-im/libopusencoder-android)). Re-streaming decoded audio may also raise licensing questions that were not researched.

The deciding point is that host streaming solves content identity, not output timing. Once the YouTube itag is pinned, self-fetched streams are already byte-identical, and the per-device output-latency problem remains in both modes. Host streaming is therefore a **feature for offline guests and a fallback when a guest's file mismatches**, not the route to tighter sync. It only becomes natural after the sample-accurate PCM output path exists, because the same "play frame N at host time T" sink serves both sources.

## Prioritized recommendations for SongSync

The table orders the work by expected improvement per unit of effort. Phase 0 comes first because three open questions decide which later items matter: how far ExoPlayer's position differs from the acoustic output, whether phones actually get different files, and how stable the clock is on real links.

|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Add a "measure only" acoustic check (reuse `CalibrationAnalyzer`, apply nothing), per-phone format/decoder logging, and clock-quality stats | Real error sources are invisible to the follower; the sim assumes unbiased positions | S–M |
| 1 | Pin the YouTube itag (and content length) in `Track` as JioSaavn pins `bitrateKbps` | itag 140 vs 251 are not aligned; offsets of 6.5–60 ms are possible ([audiomisc](https://audiomisc.co.uk/YouTube/SpotTheDifference.html)) | S |
| 2 | Remove the distance bias from echo calibration (placement UX now, two-way ranging later) | 2.9 ms per metre of host-mic path is baked into the correction | S / L |
| 3 | Hold a low-latency Wi-Fi lock during sessions; reset the clock window on bandwidth change | Power save adds ≥100 ms asymmetric delay ([AOSP](https://source.android.com/docs/core/connect/wifi-low-latency)) | S |
| 4 | Gentler correction: max nudge 0.5% for small errors, finer step; keep 2% only for catch-up | Snapcast caps at 0.05%; smaller steps let the loop settle tighter | S |
| 5 | Microsecond position from a forwarding audio sink; then dead-band 4 → 1.5–2 ms | `currentPosition` is ms-quantised and smoothed | M |
| 6 | Offset+skew clock estimator with an error bound | 1–80 ppm skew; a 20 s window lags | M |
| 7 | Own the PCM output (AudioTrack first, Oboe optional): silence-pad to the target frame, insert/drop frames at ≤0.05%, Snapcast thresholds | The only proven route to ≤1 ms (Snapcast <0.2 ms) | L |

## Conclusion

SongSync's remaining error sits mostly outside the sync algorithm. The engine is already structurally right, so tuning its constants against `currentPosition` will plateau around today's few milliseconds, because that number is a smoothed, millisecond-quantised estimate of what the speaker is doing. Two cheap changes probably matter more than any constant: pinning the YouTube stream, and taking flight time out of the echo calibration. Each can silently introduce 5–60 ms errors that the closed loop reports as zero. The step to "excellent" (≤1–2 ms) is architectural: own the audio output so that the start and every correction are expressed in frames against a hardware timestamp, as Snapcast does.

There is also a ceiling. With phones metres apart, geometry alone produces several milliseconds of spread for most listeners. Below about 1 ms of device error, improvements become measurable but rarely audible. The practical target is ≤2 ms device-to-device emission error, verified acoustically on real phones. That gives SongSync a useful property: every gain can be proven with the microphone it already uses.
